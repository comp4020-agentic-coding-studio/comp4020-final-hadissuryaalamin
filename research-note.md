# Match Agent Architectures to Task Requirements

Good agentic software-development practice should select multi-agent architectures according to task requirements, and justify their use through measurable benefits that survive coordination, integration, and rework. Selection should begin with the task's quality requirements, the separability of its parts, dependencies between decisions, and the coordination needed to maintain consistency. Evaluating the integrated result then establishes whether distributing work actually serves those requirements; agent count alone cannot establish progress.

[Kim et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3) provide evidence for conditional selection: coordinated agents improve financial reasoning, whereas all tested multi-agent architectures achieve lower average task-success rates than the single-agent system on SWE-bench Verified. Their single-agent comparator already iterates, with reported matching of maximum total iterations and reasoning-token budgets. However, the software evaluation covers only 20 instances, with wide confidence intervals, and finance gains cannot be transferred directly to coding. Task dependencies offer a plausible explanation for contrasting outcomes, but the study does not isolate their causal effect. Some models performed better with multiple agents. These results do not show that single agents are always better for coding. Instead, the choice of architecture should depend on which parts of the task can run independently and which need coordination.

Separability also requires more than assigning different files. Components may depend on shared assumptions about interfaces, behaviour, or state. [Yan (2026)](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working) describes conflicting implicit decisions among parallel coding agents, while [Cemri et al. (2025, §4)](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3) identify inter-agent misalignment and inadequate task verification in execution traces. They describe verifiers checking whether code compiles but not whether it works as required. Most trace labels come from a validated LLM annotator, and this observational evidence neither explains Kim's specific outcomes nor establishes single-agent superiority. Together, these accounts suggest that decomposition creates obligations to communicate consequential decisions and verify combined behaviour. Parallel coding may suit independent tasks with stable interfaces. Closely connected tasks may need fewer coding agents or more frequent communication.

Yet consistency does not require every agent to share identical context. Yan reports useful coding and review loops in which a separate reviewer begins without the coder's conversation history, while findings are reconciled with the overall instructions. One writer with a separate reviewer is therefore an illustrative option when independent scrutiny matters, not a universal design rule. Although some verifiers failed in Cemri's study, separate reviewers may still be useful. Conversely, Yan's production account supplies no controlled comparison and promotes Cognition's commercial products. It offers a design possibility to evaluate, rather than proof of superiority.

The strongest counter-case is that parallel exploration and separate contexts may improve quality or shorten delivery enough to warrant higher expenditure. Coordination failures may also be repairable: [Cemri et al. (2025, Appendix H.2)](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3) improve ChatDev through prompt and workflow interventions, although the restricted settings cannot establish general benefits. Better orchestration can change the comparison, so a poor initial result warrants diagnosis rather than automatic abandonment. These possibilities qualify the argument: measurable benefit need not mean lowest cost, and quality, time, and expenditure should receive different weights according to the task. Equal-cost comparisons ask what performance a budget buys; equal-time comparisons ask what can be achieved within a given duration. Neither answers every engineering decision.

My practical recommendation is to compare plausible architectures on representative tasks, including a capable iterative single agent where relevant. Identify which work can proceed independently, which dependencies require coordination, and what counts as successful completion. Assess final requirement satisfaction and elapsed time through integration and verification. Record actual token consumption and API charges across workers, coordinators, reviewers and retries, alongside relevant tool and compute costs. Account for integration and rework burdens where they affect the decision; budget allowances do not establish actual consumption. This is proportionate evaluation, not exhaustive benchmarking. Select the architecture whose quality, time, and cost trade-offs fit the task and whose benefits remain after the pieces are combined.
